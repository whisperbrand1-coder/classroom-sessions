DEFINITION

What is information security? 🔐

Information security means protecting information from unauthorized access, unwanted changes, and service disruption. It protects three things:

🔐 Confidentiality✅ Integrity🟢 Availability
🎓 Example: a university protects exam results so unauthorized users cannot view or change them — and so they are available during results day.

The CIA Triad

Three goals sit at the heart of every security decision. We'll use one story — school grades — for all three. 🎓

🔐 Confidentiality✅ Integrity🟢 Availability

Three goals, one triangle

🔐

Confidentiality

Only authorized people can access information.

✅

Integrity

Information stays accurate and unchanged.

🟢

Availability

Systems are accessible when needed.

🎓 Shared example: student grades should be private (C), correct (I), and available during registration or exams (A).
🔐 C

Confidentiality

Keeping private information away from unauthorized people.

Examples of private data

🩺 Medical records🎓 Student grades💰 Salaries🔑 Passwords

Methods that protect it

🚪 Access control🔒 Encryption💪 Strong passwords📲 MFA
🏫 Example: only school admins should access private student records — that is "need-to-know" access.
✅ I

Integrity

Keeping data accurate, complete and unchanged — unless an authorized person changes it.

Must not change without permission

Methods that protect it

📜 Audit logs✍️ Digital signatures🔗 Hashes🚪 Permissions
🧾 Example: if a grade is changed, the system should record who changed it and when.
🟢 A

Availability

Systems and data should work when users need them.

When it really matters

🏫 Registration day🏥 Hospital emergencies🏦 Banking apps

Methods that protect it

💾 Backups➕ Redundancy📈 Monitoring🚨 Incident response
🖥️ Example: if one server fails, a backup server keeps the service running.

CHAPTER 3 · NEVER STOPS 🔄

The Information Security Lifecycle

Security is a loop, not a one-time install. Five phases repeat forever: Identify → Protect → Detect → Respond → Recover.

One repeating loop

🔁 After Recover, you feed lessons back into Identify. Security never "finishes".
🔍 IDENTIFY

Know what you must protect

The Identify phase finds your assets, critical data, systems, users & roles, plus threats, vulnerabilities and risks. First, learn six words that professionals never mix up:

WordMeaningSchool-database example
💎 AssetSomething valuable to protectThe student database
👾 ThreatWho/what could cause harmAn attacker
🕳️ VulnerabilityA weakness that can be exploitedWeak password / weak access control
⚠️ RiskChance a threat exploits a weaknessUnauthorized access & data theft
💥 ImpactThe damage if it happensPrivacy damage & loss of trust
🛡️ ControlWhat we add to reduce the riskMFA, permissions, encryption, monitoring
🏥 Example: a hospital identifies patient records as highly sensitive — exposing them harms privacy and trust.

What each phase actually does

🛡️

Protect

Passwords, firewalls, training, backups, access control, encryption & policies.

📡

Detect

Logs & monitoring spot repeated failed logins, unusual traffic or odd behavior.

🚑

Respond

Disable a suspicious account, isolate a device, stop the activity, limit damage.

🔧

Recover

Restore clean backups, patch weaknesses, restore services & improve defenses.

📡 Detect example: a monitoring system spots repeated failed logins on a staff account. 🚑 Respond: IT disables that account. 🔧 Recover: restore backups, patch, update procedures.

CHAPTER 4 · RULES & WALLS 🧱

Safeguards, Classification & Governance

Three kinds of safeguards, four levels of data, and who makes the rules versus who applies them.

Human · Physical · Technical 🧱

🧑‍🏫

Human

Training, awareness & policies.

🎣 Phishing training
🚪

Physical

Real-world protections.

🔒 Locks · 🪪 badges · 📹 cameras
💻

Technical

Software & hardware controls.

🧱 Firewalls · 🛡️ antivirus · 🔒 encryption
🧩 Strong security layers all three — technology alone cannot fix a careless click or an unlocked server room.

Data classification levels 🗂️

Not all data needs the same protection. Label it, then protect the sensitive levels hardest.

🔴 Restricted — top secret. Example: admin passwords.
🟠 Confidential — private info. Example: student grades.
🔵 Internal — for organization use. Example: class schedules.
🟢 Public — anyone can see. Example: the public school website.
WHO DOES WHAT

Governance vs. IT Security

🏛️

Security Governance

Sets the rules. Creates policies, sets accountability, defines responsibility, and gets leadership support.

🔧

IT Security

Implements the rules. Configures tools & systems, applies technical controls, and monitors.

Simple version: 🏛️ Governance creates the rules. 🔧 IT Security implements the rules. Example: the principal sets a "must be password-protected" rule; the IT team enforces it and auditors check it.

CHAPTER 6 · DECIDE WISELY ⚠️

Risk Assessment

You can't fix everything at once. Risk assessment helps teams decide what to protect first using likelihood and impact.

DEFINITION

What is risk assessment? ⚠️

It helps organizations identify and evaluate cybersecurity risks by answering five questions:

What needs protection? 💎
What could go wrong? 👾
How likely is the problem? 🎲
How serious would the damage be? 💥
What should we do first? 🥇
🏫 Example: a school app stores student records, attendance and parent contacts. If this data is exposed, the impact could be serious — so it ranks high.

Defining your protection scope 🎯

Scope = deciding which parts of a system must be secured, so nothing critical is missed.

🗄️

Student records DB

Sensitive personal & academic data.

🌐

Public web pages

Visible to everyone online.

✉️

Staff email

Internal communication.

🕘

Attendance system

Tracks student presence.

🔎 A clear scope keeps security effort on high-value areas and prevents missing critical parts.
HOW MUCH RISK? 🎚️

Risk appetite & prioritization

Risk appetite = how much risk an organization will accept before acting. It differs by organization and situation.

SLIDER Different orgs, different limits

🏫 School example: 🔴 database leak → fix immediately · 🟠 weak password policy → improve soon · 🟢 minor UI bug → accept temporarily.

CHAPTER 8 · DIGITAL FINGERPRINTS 🔗

Hashing & Evidence Integrity

How do we prove evidence wasn't changed? We take a hash — a digital fingerprint that changes completely if even one byte changes.

Protecting evidence integrity 🛡️

📄

1 · Create a copy

Never work on the original — duplicate it first.

🔗

2 · Calculate a hash

A SHA-256 fingerprint of the copy.

🚫

3 · Write-protect

Read-only, so nothing can change it.

📝

4 · Document

Record every step and tool used.

🔬 Match the hash before and after analysis. If they match, the evidence stayed unchanged.
LIVE LAB 🔬

Hash laboratory

Set a baseline hash, then change a single character and watch the fingerprint change completely.

SHA-256 Real browser hashing

DON'T CONFUSE THEM 🔀

Hashing vs. encryption

🔗 Hashing = a one-way fingerprint for verification (not meant to be reversed). 🔒 Encryption = reversible with the correct key.

CHAPTER 9 · MATH THAT PROTECTS 🔑

Cryptography

The science of protecting information with math — keeping it private, proving it wasn't changed, and confirming who sent it.

DEFINITION

What is cryptography? 🔑

Cryptography protects information using mathematical techniques. It supports four goals:

🤫

Confidentiality

Only authorized users can read it.

✅

Integrity

The data was not changed.

🪪

Authentication

Proves who sent the message.

✍️

Non-repudiation

The sender can't deny it later.

🔐 Example: when you log in to a website, cryptography protects your password as it travels from your browser to the server.
METAPHOR 🧰

The digital locked box

Cryptography is like a locked box: only the right person can open it, check it, or prove where it came from. Click to explore each goal.

▸🤫 Confidentiality
Only authorized users can read the data — encryption keeps the content secret.
▸✅ Integrity
The data was not changed — hashes & signatures detect tampering.
▸🪪 Authentication
Proves who sent the message — like a digital signature from your bank.
▸✍️ Non-repudiation
The sender cannot deny a properly signed action later.
TRY IT 🔒

Encryption & decryption

Encryption turns readable plaintext into unreadable ciphertext. Only the correct key can decrypt it.

SYMMETRIC One shared key (like AES)

📦 Encryption is used in messaging apps, device storage, websites, file protection & payment systems.

Two kinds of encryption 🔑

🔑

Symmetric

One shared secret key encrypts & decrypts. Fast & great for large data. Both sides must know the key.

Example: AES
🔑🗝️

Asymmetric

Public key encrypts, private key decrypts. Great for secure key exchange & identity. Slower.

Example: RSA · ECC
📝 Correction: the original slide labelled the public/private-key method "Symmetric" — that's a typo. Public + private key = Asymmetric. Neither is simply "stronger": they solve different problems and are often used together (asymmetric to exchange a key, symmetric to encrypt the data).

ASYMMETRIC Public locks · private unlocks

BROWSER SECURITY 🔒

HTTPS & the padlock

HTTPS uses TLS to encrypt traffic between your browser and a website — protecting passwords, messages & payments. Certificates & certificate authorities build a chain of trust.

⚠️ Important: the padlock means the connection is encrypted — it does not prove the website itself is honest or safe.
